# Measurement audit: conclusions and verification boundaries

The headline observational differences reproduce as simple summaries of the released analytical variables. The strongest measurement concerns are shared tenure being labeled resignation, extreme cohort concentration of missing caste-land data, institutional ambiguity in the unopposed question, and outcomes split from the same citizen questions. The proposed one-third benchmark is informative only with qualifications: the instrument permits additional participants, and the paper itself already discusses the 31% speaking share as evidence that direct presidents are not autocrats.

This read-only slice reviewed the article, appendix, deposited elite/citizen instruments, codebook, and the producing R code, and independently tabulated the three observational CSV files using Python's standard library. It did not execute or change estimators. Numerical inference belongs to the main audit pipeline. See the [literal dictionary](variable-dictionary.md) for questions, denominators, exact category counts and source locations.

## Criticisms to narrow or reject

**Less administrative exposure for direct councils is not established and could have the wrong direction.** Collection occurred in summer 2022. If the reform timeline maps to the ongoing terms in the administrative census, direct terms originating during July 2017–January 2020 would generally be older than indirect terms originating in 2020–2021. This would imply greater, not smaller, direct-cohort exposure and would work against explaining its lower cumulative resignation frequency through a shorter observation window. The administrative election dates are not released, so this is a conditional rival check, not a verified age distribution or an adjusted estimate. The separate survey sample cannot supply those missing administrative dates. In the survey, collection over 2020–2022 further means older election cohorts may have been interviewed earlier; a single assumed endpoint cannot establish their time at risk either.

**Parity does not refute an increase in authority.** The released president shares move from 25.864% to 31.060% (speaking) and 17.838% to 32.906% (most influential). Movement toward one-third is compatible with the paper's comparative claim. Moreover, the most-influential question includes none, relative and other-participant categories. The paper p. 13, footnote 32 explicitly notes 31% speaking and says direct presidents are not autocrats. Report the benchmark as a descriptive comparison; do not accuse the paper of claiming universal or absolute executive dominance based on these means.

**The two citizen categories are not exact complements.** The remaining categories carry substantial probability mass, so an increase in current-president responses need not be an equal decrease in the paper's outside-elite grouping. Reconstruct and show the residual instead of calling the second coefficient algebraically redundant.

**Resignations are not unsupported as an elite tactic.** Appendix D.2.6 reports 71/87=81.61% of follow-up respondents attributed rotation decisions to influential villagers; this arithmetic is consistent with 82%. The 92 positive president reports in the released data match the stated contact frame. Qualitative evidence and the selected follow-up support the existence of the tactic. They do not validate negative reports, establish a common exposure window, or show that every case is elite-directed. Some tabulated cases concern disqualification, job opportunities, no-confidence motions or ward-member decisions.

**Unopposed-election ambiguity is acknowledged in the paper.** P. 15 states that the available data cannot test whether uncontested elections reflect elite manipulation. Appendix D.3 explores the competing higher-stakes account using voting reports; this does not resolve selection-stage comparability. The audit should challenge the measurement inference, not claim that the author ignored the alternative altogether.

**The paper's formal-capture mechanism is not a demonstrated mediation effect.** P. 16 and footnote 44 explicitly state that the vignette cannot identify direct-election effects mediated by formal capture. Its randomized caste/gender contrast concerns perceptions of hypothetical presidents in a fixed Maratha-majority village, not actual behavior or realized institutional mediation. This limitation is already conceded; any stronger claim should be tied to exact contrary prose rather than treating the concession as absent.

## Untestable diagnostics and minimum missing artifacts

| Requested diagnostic | Missing artifact |
|---|---|
| Full influential-actor categories | Original six-category discussion response; attendance roster and coding instructions |
| Speaking distribution across actors | Actor-specific shares or recordings/timestamps and denominator definition; these are not in the deposited instrument excerpt |
| Shared-tenure plans versus completed turnover | Original four-category Q15, dates of changes, interview date, verified council term-start date |
| Administrative risk/survival model | Administrative term-start, extraction/censoring and resignation dates; preferably council ID and data-entry documentation |
| District/taluka clustering and fixed effects | Anonymized district/taluka codes with verified council crosswalk |
| Full citizen multinomial distributions | Original Q1/Q2 categories and raw-to-binary cleaning program |
| Rich-landowner existence versus influence, elected versus external status | Separate Q16/Q17 responses, skip logic, landowner elected-status information |
| Caste-land missingness by geography | Geographic codes; explanation of item administration by wave/cohort and original land assessment inputs |

Anonymization does not require revealing village names to supply cluster IDs, elapsed months or category codes. No contact with authors or other people was made in this measurement slice.
