1     Overview
Not all politicians are equally able to influence decision-making processes and policy
outcomes (Tolentino and Cruz 2022; Purohit 2021; Heinze, Brulé and Chauchard 2024).
When dominant groups with various sources of social privilege capture political institu-
tions through elected office, this may lead them to accrue an authority advantage. This
survey experiment builds on existing literatures on elite capture and political inequality
by focusing on two factors that may affect leaders’ authority: leader gender and social
class (here: caste).
        I employ a survey experiment with a 2x2 factorial design that randomly varies the
gender and social class of hypothetical local politicians in order to probe their causal im-
pact on political authority, pliability, and backlash in a face-to-face survey of 2340 voters
in Maharashtra, India. This pre-analysis plan was written after project implementation,
but before data was received by the author.1


2     Pilot results
The experimental stimuli and outcome questions were piloted from 12-13 July 2024 in
Jalgaon district among a gender- and caste-balanced sample of 10 citizens, 5 living in
the Jalgaon City Assembly constituency and 5 in Jalgaon Rural Assembly Constituency,
all familiar with rural politics. It was also piloted on 22 July 2024 in the Jalgaon Rural
Constituency among 4 voters.
        Piloting served three purposes: manipulation checks of the gender and caste
variables, construct validity of the outcome variables, and development of options for
outcome variables. Table 1 summarizes how the results of the pilot study have informed

3     Design

3.1   Study population, sampling, survey design

2340 voters above the age of 18 were surveyed across 18 state assemblies in North Ma-
harashtra. The survey used a stratified random sampling strategy for surveying voters.
100 voters from each of the 18 constituencies, and then an additional 340 voters in the
3 urban centers were sampled to ensure a balance of rural and urban voters; additional
Dalit voters in the Bhusawal constituency and other constituencies were sampled, for a
total targeted sample of 2340 voters. The exact gender and ethnic stratification of vot-
ers across the 18 constituencies are included in Appendix 1. Within each constituency,




                                               3
one village or town was selected per 20 respondents, for a total of 4 to 6 sites per con-
stituency, while for the urban centers, all the respondents are sampled in the same city.
These sites are selected based on their ethnic composition and areas where grassroots
organizations are active in voter mobilization. To achieve the stratified quota sample, a
random walk method was employed where enumerators turn left when entering the area
and survey every fourth individual until the desired gender and ethnic quota within the
location is met. No payment was provided for participation in the survey. The survey
took place from late August 2024 to mid October 2024.
      The survey included several experimental modules after pre-treatment covariates.
The order of the experimental modules – and thus of the experimental module of interest
in this study – was randomized.


3.2   Treatment groups and randomization

The experiment features a vignette of a hypothetical village president, where the gender
and caste of the president vary, with two possibilities each (2x2 factorial design). Par-
ticipants are randomly assigned using simple randomization (with equal probabilities of
assignment to condition) to 1 of 4 conditions, displayed in Table 2.

      In particular, gender of the president was manipulated using she/her or he/him
pronouns combined with an obviously female (Bharti) or male (Rohit) first name in
this context. Caste of the president was manipulated using surnames with obvious
caste connotations in this context: Marathe (Maratha) or Kamble (Dalit). Three other
dimensions are kept constant: the age of the president (the mean age of the president

5    Hypotheses and analysis strategy
This section details four main and two secondary hypotheses to be tested, as well as
two exploratory analyses. All quantitative analyses will be run via OLS regression,
with Huber-White heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors, and statistical significance
will be assessed at the 5 % level. I will report covariate-adjusted estimates – my pre-
ferred models – in the main paper and unadjusted difference in means estimates in the
appendix.

       Finally, I will perform two exploratory analyses. First, I expect gender dominance
and caste dominance to interact, with order of advantage being Maratha men, Maratha
women, Dalit men, Dalit women for authority and pliability outcomes. However, this
design is underpowered to detect interaction effects, so I will display bar graphs with
means for each identity category as a suggestive analysis. Second, I will investigate the
qualitative responses to questions 1, 2 and 3, that follow up to ask ”why” respondents
gave the response that they did.


5.1   Covariates and balance

In the appendix, I will report balance for the following covariates: locality (urban vs.
rural), age, gender, religion, caste, education, prior voter participation, self-reported
knowledge on local politics, and a gender norms indicator. These are the covariates that
I will include in my regression models.
       The covariates will be coded as follows:

  • Locality (rural area): Factor variable with two levels: Village and Nagar palika or
      Mahanagar palika.

  • Age: continuous variable.




                                           10
  • Gender: Factor variable with two levels: Male, female.

  • Religion: Factor variable with seven levels: Hindu, Muslim, Christian, Buddhist,
      Sikh, Adviasi, and Jain. This is subject to adjustement based on the sample
      demographics.

  • Caste: Factor variable with six levels: Adivasi, Dalit, OBC, Maratha, VJNT,
      and other general caste. This is subject to adjustement based on the sample
      demographics.

  • Education: Factor variable with three levels: Primary school or less, secondary
      school, undergraduate and above. This is subject to adjustement based on the
      sample demographics.

  • Prior voter participation: Factor variable with two levels: voted in 2019 state
      elections or did not vote.

  • Self-reported knowledge on local politics: Factor variable with two levels: Not
      knowledgeable at all or reports having some knowledge.

  • Gender norms: The value given for the following question:

        – As per your understanding, approximately what percent (on a scale of 0
           to 100) of the inhabitants in your locality think that it is appropriate that
           women vote? (on a scale of 0 to 100)

       In the appendix, I will document analyses that probe balance. I will present
the proportion of voters and standard deviation for each covariate level across the four
treatment groups. I will assess balance using F-tests of global significance for these
covariates for each treatment group, via regressing each of the four treatment group
indicators on these covariates, extracting the F-statistic, and its p-value. Failing to
reject the null at the 5 percent level supports balance.

5.2   Power calculations

Sample size was fixed and not under control of the researcher. Care was thus taken
to increase the intensity of the treatments in order to better chances at higher levels
of power. In addition, the preferred specifications include covariate adjustment to try
to increase power as blocking was not feasible in the study design. With a sample size
of 2,340 voters, I should be able to detect effects for main hypotheses (M1 and M2) of
0.125 of a standard deviation of the outcome, setting alpha at 0.05 (power= 0.80).4
