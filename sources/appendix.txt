A       Qualitative data collection description
This section details over 2 years of qualitative data collection that contribute to this project,
both in theory-building and theory-testing.

A.1      Theory-building
From March 2018 to March 2020, I conducted theory-building, exploratory qualitative work
that led to the development of my theory and hypotheses.
    During this time period, I visited 47 village councils, 11 district offices, and 15 block
offices in the western state of Maharashtra. I employed a combination of interview-based,
observational, and shadow-based research techniques. I visited village councils (gram pan-
chayats) in 12 districts across Maharashtra; district offices (zilla parishad ) in 11 districts
(Pune, Solapur, Satara, Ahmednagar, Nashik, Aurangabad, Nanded, Washim, Amravati,
Jalgaon, Nagpur); and block offices (panchayat samiti ) in 15 blocks. On average, I visited
each district and block office once. I visited each village council between 1 and 5 times.1
    While visiting district and block offices, I interviewed government officials and politicians
who were present. This included conducting interviews about the de jure institutional design
of the village council, as well as about the de facto functioning of the village council. Visits
to village councils included a range of interactions. In every village council, at a minimum, I
interviewed and shadowed the village council president for an entire day. In addition, in some
village councils, I was also able to interview the vice president, village secretary, active ward
members, village citizens, and observe the monthly meeting, village meeting, and election
proceedings. All in all, I conducted approximately 150 interviews, and observed 20 monthly
meetings.2
    In order to understand the experiences of village council presidents, it was important
to combine both interview-based and observational methods. First, it is clear that it is
important to interview the council president directly, in order to have a first-hand account
of her own experience, as she understands it. Yet, social desirability bias may be particu-
larly acute for public officials, as they may have incentives to report only information that
conforms with the legal expectations of the institutions in which they serve. One way I
sought to triangulate information was through additional interviews with key stakeholders
(vice president, village secretary, ward members, village notables), allowing me to get at
other players’ perceptions of the village council president’s authority. Perhaps even more
importantly, shadowing and observation were key to supplementing my understanding of
the presidents’ experience within the institution of the village council. Spending multiple
days observing the council president complete her tasks allowed me to observe her inter-
actions with others in the council. Further, sitting in on village council monthly meetings

and observation-based techniques allowed me to triangulate information in order to develop
a detailed picture of the reality in each village council.
    Before going to a village council, I called the council president’s number (obtained via
public records kept either online or at the block office) beforehand to introduce myself, the
research project, and ask permission to come for an interview in her village. Upon arriving to
the village at the time and place given by the village council president, I would re-introduce
myself (and if applicable, my research associate) and re-explain my research interests. At
times, the interviews took place in the village council office building, and other times it took
place in the home of the council president. Though the interviews were largely carried out
in private, it was not uncommon for office clerks to stop by and ask about the reason for the
interview, if it was carried out in the village council office. Once I had completed the initial
interview with the council president, I asked permission to interview the village secretary,
vice president, village notables, as well as to attend any village functions, council meetings,
and observe the president during one day of work. Interviews with village secretaries either
took place in the block office or in the village council office. Interviews with vice president
were conducted either at their home or in the village council office. Interviews with village
notables were conducted either at their location of work (school, health center, day care,
etc.) or at their home. None of the participants of the any of the interviews were present
for the interviews of others. Each interview lasted between 30 minutes and 1 hour, and took
place between the hours of 8:00 a.m. and 5:00 p.m.
    In addition to interviews, I supplemented my data collection efforts with observational
research. I did this through shadowing the village council president in the village and village
council, observing her routines, actions, and interactions. I also attended village council
meetings, where I sat in the back of the room and observed the interpersonal interactions
and decision-making processes of the council. Village council meetings include long agendas
of many decisions to be made, and so participants must proceed with focus and efficiency
in order to make it through the agenda. Thus, the focused participation of the attendees,
and the long duration of the meetings, made me believe that any social desirability bias
related to my presence would decrease over time as participants became engaged in the
proceedings of the meeting. Plus, I would only attend meetings after several days spent
in and around a village council, so most participants in the meeting at that point were
accustomed to my presence. Moreover, I did not feel that my presence biased the actions
of meeting participants. If this were the case, one would expect that social desirability bias
would lead participants to act in a legally appropriate way during deliberation (specifically,
that they would let the president preside); yet, this is not what I found.

B       Data from quasi-experimental observational design
B.1     Dataset 1: Large-scale survey across rural Maharashtra
B.1.1    Sampling and survey design
604 village councils across 5 districts of the state of Maharashtra were sampled, approxi-
mately 120 villages in each of 5 districts. The survey and sampling design were developed
and implemented jointly with Rachel Brulé and Simon Chauchard.
    Stratification on president reservations for gender and caste (for SC, OBC, and open
sarpanches) was done as well as with the gender of the gram sevak (the main village bu-
reaucrat). Equal samples of village councils were targeted with each combination of these 3
traits. This means that we selected 3x2x2 = 12 different types of village councils, and aimed
to do so equally within each district. This meant oversampling councils reserved for OBCs
and SCs, given that in Maharashtra 27% of the president seats within each block council in
each electoral period are to be reserved for members of the OBCs, while another 13% are
reserved for SCs. This also meant oversampling councils with female gram sevaks, which are
approximated to be 20% of the total sampling frame. Finally, councils reserved for STs are
excluded (7% of total councils in Maharashtra).
    The sample included 5 districts spread across four regions of Maharashtra. Given the
focus on rural areas, the administrative region with the most dense urban center in India
– Mumbai/Konkan – was excluded. Out of the remaining regions, we planned to select
districts with relatively high proportions of SC population (to enable oversampling of SC
reservations, which are allocated based on population proportions), and relative proximity
to urban centers (to assist in our oversampling of female gram sevaks, who are more likely
to be sent to GPs at closer proximity to urban centers than to remote locales). We excluded
from our sampling frame districts with above average proportions of STs (greater than 10%)
to ensure that our exclusion of ST reserved seats (allocated by population proportion, as
are SC reservations) would not significantly bias our sample given the district population
distribution.
    The five districts selected include Solapur (Paschim region), Pune (Paschim region),
Aurangabad (Marathwada region), Amravati (Vidarbha region) and Ahmednagar (Nashik
region). In each district we then identified the five talukas (subdistricts) with the largest
absolute numbers of “rare” combinations within each district (in practice OBC and SC
presidents paired with female gram sevaks).
    Once these were selected, we next chose as close to 24 village councils as possible in each
taluka (since the total district N = 24 x 5 = 120). Since the blocking strategy described
above implies 12 different categories of village councils to be equally sampled, we simply
aimed at this stage to select 2 village councils in each of the 12 categories to reach this total
of 24 village councils per taluka. If and when we were able to choose between more than
two village councils in a given category and taluka, we selected randomly. If and when there
were fewer than two village councils in a given category within a taluka, we first attempted
to replace the “missing village councils” by oversampling similar GPs (GPs from the same
category) in another selected taluka in the same district. If and when we were unable to
do so, we replaced these missing village councils with “open” category village councils both


                                               10
with male and female gram sevaks within the taluka. As we proceeded, we made sure to
minimize the difference in the number of village councils sampled across talukas.
    The design included 10 surveys in each village council. We interviewed: the village
council president; vice president; village council bureaucrat; the president, vice president,
and bureaucrat jointly in a facilitated discussion; and a sample of 6 gender- and caste-based
citizens. Data collection occurred from 2020-2022 in 604 village councils. Sampling was
orthogonal to direct election status (this was not the purpose of the data collection at the
time).

B.1.3    Sampling citizens
The citizen survey targeted 6 key informants in each village council.
    Sampling strategy: while the field team interviewed available citizens, they had to
work with strict constraints regarding the key demographic characteristics of the group of
individuals they interviewed. Namely, they by design had to include a gender-balanced list
of 2 SCs (1 male, 1 female) and 4 non-SCs (2 male, 2 female). As a result, our sample of 6
citizens within each village is balanced on gender, and roughly balanced on caste (or at least
inclusive of lower castes). In order to ensure we have a comparable list of citizens across
sampled villages, we restricted our sample universe to include individuals who occupy the
following roles: School teacher, Anganwadi worker, PDS Shopkeeper, Self Help Group leader,
Doctor/Nurse/Public Health Center worker,Party worker, Informal Leader (ex. Religious

                                              11
leader or caste panchayat leader), Kotwal, government employees residing in the GP (but
not working for the GP), and Talathi (village revenue officer).
    This strategy enables us to achieve a gender-balanced subset of people who are truly
knowledgeable about local politics, representing both ends of the caste-based power spec-
trum: disadvantaged SC citizens, and more socially ”mainstream” citizens. Table B.2 below

B.1.4    Description of the group meeting structure & survey
The group meeting survey involves a traditional survey as well as close observations by
multiple members of our gender-balanced two-person survey team. This requires the research
team to gather in the same location (in the village council office) three top decision-makers in
local governance: the gram sevak (appointed government bureaucrat), vice president (elected
member of the village council who is appointed to be the second-in command-of the council)
and the president (the head of the council), after they have each been individually interviewed,
at a time agreed upon in advance by all.
    The group meeting is meant to approximate the dynamics of the masik sabha, which
is a monthly meeting held with only members of the village council (the elected village
council with one appointed government bureaucrat). This is the most relevant forum for
core governance decision-making related to the financial and policy-relevant administration
of the village council and the village more broadly. Much like our group meeting, the masik
sabha is not open to the public; this is where village council members make decisions about
the council’s budget and consequent policies. As such, it is an essential meeting for decision-
making with real policy implications.
    Compared to the more extensively studied gram sabha, a public village meeting that
occurs 2 to 4 times per year, we find the masik sabha to be comparatively more significant
because of its content, frequency, and context. Specifically, the masik sabha is where most
gram panchayat decisions are actually made related to budget spending and to the alloca-
tion of contracts; it is the local government meeting that occurs most regularly; and it is
a non-public context limited to village council members. Indeed, while general policy pref-
erences of villagers are discussed in the gram sabha, specific policy prerogatives and fiscal
implementation processes are developed and decided upon by the village council members
in the masik sabha.
    In the lead-up to the group meeting, the research team proceeds as such: all three
participants are individually approached (by phone) by the research team and asked to
participate in a private, individual interview; at the end of this individual interview, they
are then asked to participate in a “discussion during which the research team will be collecting
data on the development priorities of the village and on the role that the different actors
play in this process.” The group meeting simulates the structure of a typical masik sabha in
the way that the meeting had a clear set of priorities, an agenda, and the opportunity for
contribution and discussion of all members - all features of the actual monthly meeting.

B.2.1    The digitization process
Each village council’s folder was standardized in its contents. Over the course of 5 weeks,
I and two research assistants manually entered the data from the contents of each village
council’s folder. Files were organized in cabinets according to administrative blocks. In order
to avoid systematic coder bias, we flipped a coin each day to determine which cabinet each
coder would work on (that way, each coder did not systematically work on certain blocks,
thus if there are coder-specific fixed effects in coding mistakes, they are likely to be randomly
distributed.) In addition, I randomly selected 3 village council files from the piles that each
coder was working on at the end of each day to cross-check for data-entry errors and correct
any mistakes. I did not routinely uncover data-entry errors during this exercise, giving me
confidence in the training and capacities of the coders.

C.1     The history of political decentralization in Maharashtra
Maharashtra has a long history of village council elections, with some village governments
established as early as 1869 (in what was then British-ruled Bombay Province). Prior to
the reorganization of the Indian states in 1956 and the subsequent division of the Bombay
state into contemporary Gujarat and Maharashtra, rural local bodies were governed largely
at the whims of local elites. Sometimes elections were held regularly, and other times they
were not. Village councils were also established at differing times (see next section) – with
some established in the colonial era, and others forming ever since.
    It was not until 1958, with the landmark Bombay Village Panchayats Act, that a unified
law was put forth to govern all of the region’s local governments. This act sought to specify
and standardize the functioning of village councils across the state elections - positing, for
example, that village council elections take place every four years, and that each revenue
village or group of revenue villages have its own village government. This marked the first
attempt to standardize Maharashtra’s electoral cycles, fixing them at four years. Despite
the passing of the landmark Bombay Village Panchayats Act in 1958, scholars note that its
implementation was at times piecemeal.
    The early 1990s brought the strongest attempt at standardization of village governments:
with the passage of national legislation that sought to strengthen local governments across
India. In particular, the state-appointed Patil Committee as well as the Bongirwar Commit-
tee compiled reports in the run-up to the 73rd constitutional amendment that highlighted
the shortcomings of the current local government system in Maharashtra. The Patil Com-
mittee noted in 1986 the need for systematic elections to take place and be orchestrated
by independent election machinery. Prior to this, the Bongirwar Committee noted the diffi-
culty of the current election system - and particularly the fact that elections not only did not
happen regularly across village councils, but also did not happen in a simultaneous fashion
across the state. They recommended that “elections to [village councils]... should be held at
a stretch and almost simultaneously,” but the state government did not accept this recom-
mendation. Moreover, national reformers decided that all states should hold village council
elections every five years, beginning from the time that their State Election Commissions
would be set up and ready to conduct elections.
    According to bureaucrats at the top of the hierarchy of the State Election Commission,
state leaders decided to continue to hold asynchronous elections in 1995 for political reasons.
Plus, they were able to do so because the 73rd Constitutional Amendment devolved the de-
cisions around the specifics of election administration to states. In the early 1990s, Congress
– the state’s historically dominant party – was facing an existential crisis of dwindling sup-
port and, particularly, a shrinking rural voter base. Crucially, Congress was implementing
Maharashtra’s version of the 73rd amendment requirements in the context of upcoming 1995
State Assembly elections. State leaders feared that if they dissolved all rural bodies, they
would lose crucial rural support in the lead-up to the Assembly elections. As a result, they
agreed to hold elections every five years, but decided to do so asynchronously – maintaining
the state’s impressively staggered election schedule. This is in contrast to the neighboring
state of Karnataka, which decided to dissolve all rural bodies and hold synchronous village


                                              21
council elections in the same period.3
    In all, there were three distinct periods of electoral cycles in Maharashtra until contempo-
rary times, but electoral cycles were initially determined with differing establishment dates.
Pre-1958, elections were held sporadically, according to the whims of local leaders. Between
1995 and 1958, elections were held at 4-year intervals, though historical accounts suggest
that this was not universally respected across the state. Finally, from 1995 onwards, elec-
tions were with more regularity at 5-year intervals. While establishment dates determined
the initial “path” of a village council, other village-level idiosyncracies, exogenous events,
and bureaucratic arbitrariness pushed some councils further off their paths.

C.2       Analysis: Establishment dates and electoral cycles
As noted above, village councils in Maharashtra were established at varying times, and this
set the foundation for varying electoral cycles. Figure C.1 below shows the distribution of
establishment dates from the villages in the survey data used in this paper. The first red
line is at 1958, with the first electoral cycle reform (elections every 4-years). The second
red line is at 1995, with the second electoral cycle regime (elections every 5-years). Village
councils were established before 1958, between 1958 and 1995, and after 1995.
    Establishment years are predictive of the latest election cycle recorded in the survey
data, suggesting that they play a key role in setting the foundation for today’s electoral
cycles (shown in Table C.1.). However, the mapping is not one to one: establishment dates
do not fully predict current electoral cycles, i.e. they are not deterministic. This provides
additional evidence that due to additional exogenous events or bureaucratic reasons, some
village councils deviated from their initial paths.

indicators for establishment dates are, however, balanced across direct and indirect councils,

C.8     Partisan targeting
While – as discussed in the main paper – the direct election reform likely had political
motivations on a macro-scale, evidence does not support that its implementation in specific
village councils was targeted in a partisan manner as the micro-level.
     The reform was implemented at the village-level as soon as it was passed at the state-level,
in the monsoon session of the state legislature in 2017. Election bureaucrats confirm that
it is not the case, for example, that an implementation lag occurred wherein certain village
councils were able to hold their elections indirectly after the passage of the law. As soon
as the Maha Vikas Aghadi coalition (an alliance between the Shiva Sena, National Congress
Party, and Indian National Congress) came to power at the end of 2019, they moved to
reinstate indirect elections, which was passed and implemented promptly in January 2020.
Even though this is not the reform period under study, as soon as the BJP-led coalition
came into power again in June 2022, they passed and implemented a reform to move back
to direct elections by the end of that summer. These behaviors and their timing suggest
that each party had developed a clear preference around which village council elections it
preferred, and moved to realize these preferences as early as possible within their time in
office, rather than, for instance, strategically timing the passage of the electoral reforms so
that certain councils would receive certain electoral regimes.
     Indeed, a preference of the BJP over the type of electoral system does not imply that it
could allocate the system to particular villages, in a way that would violate as-if random.
Interviews with BJP party leaders suggests that the party did not have discretion over where
the reform was implemented when it was passed in 2017. Engaging in partisan “targeting”
in this manner would require tracking the state’s impressively staggered elections, matching
election timings to places where the BJP lacked a strong base, and intentionally timing the
passage of the reform such that it would be implemented in only these places (or, alterna-
tively, selectively implementing the reform in these places). Interviewees report a general
inability to interfere in the Election Commission’s independent election implementation pro-
cess as well as the lack of incentive to do so: perceived election manipulation would not bode
well for electoral support, they explained. Plus, they averred the BJP’s incentive was not to
target the reform only to certain places, but rather to implement it broadly across the state
as it had become a part of the party’s brand in promoting efficiency and development. In
addition, the lack of an ability to target and the lack of incentive to do so are compounded
by the fact that direct and indirect villages in the survey sample do not exhibit differential
citizen preferences for the BJP (post-treatment), which I show in Appendix D.3. Appendix
D.3 demonstrates similar political party support across both direct and indirect villages, as
well as statistically indistinguishable citizen support for the BJP and Congress across direct
and indirect villages. This analysis of citizen partisan support are post-treatment and thus
require caveating. To the extent that partisan identities are sticky, one may expect that
if the BJP successfully targeted direct elections’ implementation in places with differential
party support, this would be detectable after the reform as well.

D.1.5   Robustness 4: Probing the role of unobservables through sensitivity anal-
        ysis
I use the sensemakr package in R, developed by Cinelli and Hazlett (2020) to probe the
role unobservables could play in confounding effects. Figure D.1 below plots the sensitivity
of the t-values associated with the estimates of main effects (for the five de facto author-
ity outcomes, using linear regressions), using the gender quota variable as a benchmark to
understand how strong unobservables would have to be to significantly change the interpre-
tation of this paper’s results. In the plots below, the red line indicates a t-value of 2, the
conventional approximate value for 95 % confidence intervals. Each of the red dots plots hy-
pothetical unobserved confounders as strong as, twice as strong as, and three times as strong
as the gender quota variable. For each of the authority outcomes, the plots demonstrate that
unobserved confounders would have to have more than three times the strength of the gender
quota variable for the results of this paper to become statistically insignificant. This seems
implausible, as gender quotas are extremely theoretically and empirically important in this
context in terms of explaining authority outcomes.

D.1.6      Robustness 5: Additional qualitative evidence
This appendix documents additional qualitative evidence from the first round of theory-
testing fieldwork that complements the empirical analyses in the main paper.
    These findings map well onto evidence from interview data. First, interview evidence
shows that village council presidents elected directly do indeed enjoy a “feeling” of de facto
authority, and they often ascribe this feeling to the way they were elected. One president
noted the importance of the sheer scale of direct elections, stating, “...I have been [directly]
elected from among 1000 people. The fact that I have been elected by thousands of people,
and not 15 members sitting at a temple gives me tremendous social power and authority.”
Another president expressed pride in being selected by the people among 6 candidates: “I
was contesting the elections with six other candidates... [and] the people chose me.” A third
president smiled as she recounted her success during the election period, showing me a photo
of her supporters hoisting her up into the air after the hotly contested direct election: “Out
of all the [presidents] elected from among the people in the entire [administrative block], I
was the one who got the largest number of votes.” She noticed the change in herself thanks
to the authority she accrued via her election: “4 years ago, I would not have been able to
speak so confidently even in front of you.” She continued, “...it’s better to be a [president]
elected from among the people... if a [president] is elected from among the people, he gets a
different kind of power from a [president] who is elected from among the members!”7
    Indirectly elected council presidents recognize these authority differentials, wishing they
would have enjoyed direct election. One president lamented feeling under pressure by elites,
stating that, conversely, “Nobody can really put pressure on a [president] elected from among
the people.” She felt disempowered by elite pressures: “They put a lot of pressure on me, but
I kept going without giving in, which is when 90 percent of the villagers stopped speaking
with me... They wouldn’t invite me to weddings, they wouldn’t call me, if I went to a
wedding, they would insult me.” Another president stated frankly that “[Being elected] from
among the people would be better, because that would mean [the president] has everyone’s
support. From among the people is ideal. If he is elected from among the members, it’s a lot
more political.” A third president was unequivocal in his opinion: “It’s better to be elected
from among the people.” When asked why he felt that way, he responded: “If you get elected
from among the people, you get a chance to actually get some work done properly.”8

D.2.5    Robustness 4: Probing the role of unobservables through sensitivity anal-
         ysis
I use the sensemakr package in R, developed by Cinelli and Hazlett (2020) to probe the
role unobservables could play in confounding effects. Figures D.2 and D.3 below plot the
sensitivity of the t-values associated with the estimates of effects (for informal and formal
capture outcomes, using linear regressions with controls), using the gender quota variable as a
benchmark to understand how strong unobservables would have to be to significantly change
the interpretation of this paper’s results. In the plots below, the red line indicates a t-value
of 2, the conventional approximate value for 95 % confidence intervals. Each of the red dots
plots hypothetical unobserved confounders as strong as, twice as strong as, and three times
as strong as the gender quota variable. For each of the outcomes, the plots demonstrate that
unobserved confounders would have to have more than three times the strength than the
gender quota variable for the results of this paper to become statistically insignificant. This
seems implausible, as gender quotas are extremely theoretically and empirically important
in this context in terms of explaining authority outcomes.

D.2.6     Robustness 5: Resignation follow-up interviews
To determine whether resignation was due to elite influence (or other reasons), semi-structured
follow-up phone interviews were conducted with all presidents in the survey that reported
an intention to resign (N = 92). 87 out of 92 responded, a response rate of 95 %. Interviews
lasted on average about 15 minutes each. Structured questions included are below, and
unstructured follow-up questions were asked to probe additional details.

     1. Did you resign from your position as president? If so, for how many years did you
        serve? How many other people served as president, and for how many years each?

     2. Please share a little bit about the reasons you decided to resign from your position.
        Was anyone else involved in this decision besides you? If so, who were the main
        decision-makers around your resignation? When and where was the decision taken?

     3. Is there anything else you would like to share about the decision to resign and your
        experience as village council president?

    Out of the 87 presidents who responded, 71 (around 82 %) shared that influential villagers
were those that decided they would resign and the conditions of their resignation. In many
instances, presidents expanded on this, sharing that elders or “big people” in the village
temple meeting made these decisions. One president’s remark is particularly illuminating:
“This system of sharing the tenure is completely inappropriate. I want to work for people but
six months are not enough. It is the bhauki -gavki (important villagers) in the villages that
forces and establishes such systems of resignation. When I became president and started
generating interest, I had to resign because I gave my word.” Table D.17 documents the
interview responses from all of the interviews.

    Informal elite capture in indirect elections: If someone wants to be considered for the post
who is not the traditional elites’ preferred candidate, one president explained, “the seniors
will request them to withdraw.” Another president described in detail how traditional elites
dominated elections in his village: “They’re in the age range of 60-70. They have a lot
of experience... they act separately from the actual voting. If I become member and you
want to become a member too, they’ll tell you to step aside for now, that they’ll give you a
chance next time. They’ll convince you that I’ll be a better choice for now... So they can
make people wait and listen to them.” “There was no competition left. 31st was the last
day to submit an application to rescind the form. After that there were 2 forms [for the
post]: mine and [another potential candidate’s]. They requested him to rescind his form by
the 4th, and on the 4th he went and got his form rescinded. And then on the 4th, I got
elected unopposed.” In short, traditional elites can craft ex ante who makes up the body via
persuasion (or coercion), and, in turn, can guarantee that who they prefer to be president
is eventually chosen.9
    Coordinated resignations occur when traditional village elites persuade or coerce council
presidents into forfeiting their office via a formal resignation. The “temple coalitions” of
“village elders” convene in an “unofficial meeting” and decide who will serve as the council
president, and for how long. When elites use this tactic, typically the term for the council
president post is divided between, at minimum, 2 people, and at maximum, 17 people. In
one village, the council president described, the village elites, “political heavyweights in [the]
village,” “sat the 6 of us down and gave each of us a tenure of around 10 months.” This
implied that each president held office for 10 months, and resigned once their 10 months
were over. Presidents described lacking the agency to resist this system, which suggests
that the resignations need not be totally voluntary. In all the interviews I conducted with
council presidents, none characterized themselves as taking part in the decision to resign.
Unanimously, they expressed a lack of ability to challenge village elites, acknowledging an
acceptance of the reality of the system.10
    One senses a feeling of disempowerment when speaking to the presidents who had to
end their time short. At the end of most interviews, I asked presidents to speak broadly
about their experience in office. One president responded: “It was nice... I learned a lot.
[Before coming into office] I didn’t know much beyond the household.11 [But] now, it’s all
gone backwards.” She and others were frank about the fact that a “backwards effect” (in
Marathi: ulta parinam) occurs: one gets just enough time in office to learn, but not enough
time to transform their status. Another president criticized the system of forced resignations:

president] needs at least 6 months to learn about the work to be done.” Yet another president,
forced to share her tenure with 5 others, recounted: “I didn’t get to learn much, because I
didn’t have much time. If I’d had more time, I would have gained more experience. I made

Another reason we may see a boost in the de facto authority of the president when she is
directly elected is because of the changing role of party involvement under the two types
of elections. Table D.18 below shows that, at least in this context, this logic does not
explain authority differentials. Column 1 shows that presidents who are directly elected
report levels of party support that are statistically indistinguishable from presidents who are
indirectly elected. Nor do directly elected presidents report receiving higher levels of party
encouragement to contest elections or party support in mobilizing voters when compared
to their indirect counterparts (Columns 2-3). Columns 4 and 5 demonstrate that directly
elected presidents are also not differentially likely to be a member of a party or have worked
for a party.
    In addition, one reason why we may expect direct elections to be more contested (and have
less instances of presidents elected in unopposed elections) is that they are higher-stakes in
general. In other words, unopposed elections under indirect elections may not indicate undue
elite influence, but rather lower-stakes elections with less attention drawn toward them. As
a potential test of this, I probe whether citizens are more likely to vote – as a proxy for the
attention the election garners – under direct and indirect elections and find no significant
differences (Column 1 of Table D.19 below). Plus, according to this logic, we would expect
to see fewer unopposed elections in indirect elections (higher political competition), because
there may be fewer barriers to entry for candidates than direct elections. The analyses in the
main paper shows the opposite: elections with a lower barrier to entry in fact have higher
rates of unopposed elections, which, I argue and qualitative evidence suggests, may be in
part due to elite coordination to fix the pool of executives.

F     Survey experiment: Design, data, analyses
F.1    Sampling
2347 voters above the age of 18 were surveyed across 18 state assemblies in North Maha-
rashtra. The survey used a stratified random sampling strategy for surveying voters. 100
voters from each of the 18 constituencies, and then an additional 340 voters in the 3 urban
centers were sampled to ensure a balance of rural and urban voters; additional Dalit voters
in the Bhusawal constituency and other constituencies were sampled, for a total targeted
sample of 2347 voters. Within each constituency, one village or town was selected per 20
respondents, for a total of 4 to 6 sites per constituency, while for the urban centers, all the
respondents are sampled in the same city. These sites are selected based on their ethnic
composition and areas where grassroots organizations are active in voter mobilization. To
achieve the stratified quota sample, a random walk method was employed where enumerators
turn left when entering the area and survey every fourth individual until the desired gender
and ethnic quota within the location is met. No payment was provided for participation in
the survey. The survey took place from late August 2024 to mid October 2024.
    The survey included several experimental modules after pre-treatment covariates. The
order of the experimental modules – and thus of the experimental module of interest in this
study – was randomized to avoid order effects.

F.2    Experimental design
The experiment features a vignette of a hypothetical village president, where the gender and
caste of the president vary, with two possibilities each (2x2 factorial design). Participants
are randomly assigned using simple randomization (with equal probabilities of assignment
to condition) to 1 of 4 conditions, displayed in Table F.1 below.

G      External validity
At the International Electoral Affairs Symposium organized by the State Election Commis-
sion of Rajasthan, I both collected expert priors about the impact of direct elections at the
local level and interviewed election experts (State Election Commissioners and other elec-
tion officials from across India) more holistically about their state’s experiences with direct
and/or indirect elections. Drawing on these two sources of data, this Appendix reasons
about the impact of direct elections outside of the case studied in this paper (Maharashtra).




                                              79
G.1     Expert predictions
This Appendix documents expert predictions about the impact of direct elections collected on
July 25, 2024 in Jodhpur, India. The priors were collected from 34 election officials (including
State Election Commissioners, Deputy Election Commissioners, Commission Secretaries)
representing 9 Indian states and Union Territories.
    In the prediction elicitation exercise, I first underscored the difference between indirect
and direct elections for the president position at the local level, though all participants
were already familiar with the institutional rules through their work. Second, participants
were given and read aloud the prompt: “Imagine: On average, indirect presidents have an
“authority” score of 20 %. On average, what would be the authority score of directly elected
presidents?” They were prompted to ask clarifying questions and discuss among themselves
if they so desired. When ready, they entered their predictions for the authority of the directly
elected president (relative to the indirectly elected president) on their mobile phones via a
mobile data collection application. Figure G.1 below plots these expert predictions, which
were calculated via subtracting 20 (the fixed, average indirect president authority score) from
the score the expert entered, to construct the predictions for the ATE of direct elections on
authority. To put the scale in terms of the authority measures used in this paper (0-1), I
divide the ATEs by 100.

    Figure G.1 show that, by and large, election experts predict large, positive effects of direct

G.2     Qualitative evidence from across India
I also interviewed several senior election officials from across the subcontinent about their
views on the impact of (in)direct elections, through their direct experience administering
elections. I briefly summarize the takeaways from these conversations here. A senior election
official from Andhra Pradesh, for instance, shared that indirect presidents have historically
been “pushed around too much,” according to his observations. The head bureaucrat of
the West Bengal Election Commission, where president elections are indirect, stated that
indirectly elected presidents typically face pressure from elite factions. These observations
are in line with the idea that indirectly elected presidents face challenges to their de facto
authority. In contrast, senior election officials from Jammu and Kashmir expressed that, in
their experience, direct elections bring in “big men” into the president position. One official
stated that typically these “influential people” like contractors, old presidents, businessmen
and landowners are the ones who win the direct president elections. Similarly in Himachal
Pradesh, the direct election of presidents has witnessed, as a senior official noted, auctions of
the president seat to the highest elite bidder. These observations are in line with this paper’s
expectations, where direct elections usher in members of the elite who firmly command
political institutions from within. In all, I take these insights from election experts across
India as suggestive evidence that the theoretical expectations of this paper may hold in
contexts outside of the one studied in this paper.

H      Is there a way out? SC quotas and direct elections
This section provides three types of evidence that probe whether SC quotas, when they
overlap with direct elections, may empower the local executive while rupturing elite cap-
ture of political institutions. First, I leverage the quasi-experimental observational design
to explore whether SC presidents, when they are directly elected, experience increased de
facto authority and decreased informal capture, while coming from outside of entrenched
elite circles. Second, I analyze the survey experimental data to explore whether hypothet-
ical SC presidents are more likely than their Maratha counterparts to be expected to face
backlash when they become powerful. Third, I draw on controlled case comparisons between
directly and indirectly elected SC-reserved villages to trace the processes of directly elected
SC president empowerment and elite backlash.

H.1     Quasi-experimental observational analysis
Using the survey and administrative data employed for the observational analyses in the main
paper, this section investigates the impact of direct elections on de facto authority, formal
capture, and informal capture among SC-reserved president seats. Figure H.1 shows these
analyses. Three suggestive findings emerge (these analyses are suggestive and underpowered
due to the small N, with 194 village councils having SC-reserved seats in the survey data).
First, SC-reserved presidents enjoy a de facto authority boost when elected directly. Second,
SC-reserved presidents, by virtue of their reservation status, do not come from Maratha caste
groups; they are also not clearly more likely to be men (this analysis is inconclusive as the
point estimate is positive, but imprecisely estimated). However, directly elected SC-reserved
presidents are more likely to benefit from class privilege, as is the case with most politicians
on a global scale. Finally, evidence suggests that directly elected SC presidents are less likely
to face some forms of informal capture, but these are effects are imprecisely estimated so
remain exploratory and suggestive.

      president] brought water. There was no street light, so she installed solar lights.”
    – Male citizen 2: “[since the] president is elected directly by the people, she holds
      the role for five years, and she can manage things properly. She can keep track of
      funds and projects... [the president] faced trouble, but a lot of development also
      took place.”
    – Female citizen 2: “[The president] has very good behavior and calmly handles
      any problem that comes up.”

• Instances of elite backlash

    – Female citizen 1: “they pressured her...People won’t say anything because if such
      things reaches to them, it could escalate.”
    – Male citizen 2: “[The harassment of the president] happens in this village... I
      can be straightforward. If anyone from a particular caste [understood as SC] is
      [president], they are not given the respect they deserve... If there’s a program in
      the village temple and the president, who happens to be a woman, goes there,
      she’s sometimes not allowed to perform the aarti (ritual offering)... Their close
      associates or supporters do the rituals instead.”
    – President: “I never had any issues with the members. But, yes, some people in
      the village do create issues.”
